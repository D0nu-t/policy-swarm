\begin{abstract}
Comparing distance measures in multidimensional bounded-confidence models involves choices that rarely reach a methods section.
We follow five of them, each met while auditing our own model: the threshold value, when coverage is matched, where the origin sits, which test is read, and how outcomes are counted.
One shared threshold admits very different shares of opinion pairs under Euclidean, cosine and angular distance, depending on how the space is coded and on its dimension.
In a minimal Deffuant model, matching coverage at the start leaves the realised interaction rates apart (median gap \VTzeroPassGap); matching it over the run equalises them (\VRunPassEquiv\ of \VCases\ cases equivalent) but not the number of opinion groups (\VRunGroupEquiv\ of \VCases), so the two matched points answer different questions, and we report whole response curves instead.
Grouping final opinions with each metric's own radius made two gates that admit the same pairs look different; one common ruler removes the difference.
In an agent-based model of AI governance, a draft finding that geometry reverses the effect of enforcement lag came from reading each geometry against a benchmark: the test that compares them keeps its sign from the draft's model to the audited one (interaction \VCDraftInter\ and \VCRecommendedInter), while the point toward which trust shrinks the perceived policy decided where the cosine index fell and, for one of four defensible targets, whether the geometries differ at all.
We close with a six-item audit list for studies that compare distance measures.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Bounded-confidence (BC) models let two agents influence each other only when their opinions are close enough \citep{deffuant2000,hegselmann2002}.
With several opinion dimensions, ``close enough'' needs a distance, and the choice has become a modelling decision in its own right: Euclidean distance is the usual default \citep{lorenz2007}, but norms other than the Euclidean \citep{lorenz2008}, angular measures \citep{schweighofer2020} and topic-weighted discordances \citep{li2026} have all been studied, often with the argument that the choice changes what the model predicts.

Comparing distance measures in a simulation involves choices that rarely appear in a paper's methods section, yet each can create an effect or hide one.
This note is about five of them, every one of which we met while auditing our own model:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{the threshold value} given to each distance measure;
\item \textbf{when coverage is matched}: at the start of a run or over the whole run;
\item \textbf{where the origin sits}, for angular gates and for any step that shrinks a quantity toward zero;
\item \textbf{which test is read}: each condition against zero, or the comparison itself;
\item \textbf{how outcomes are counted}: the radius used to group final opinions, and when the run is stopped.
\end{enumerate}

The first is not new in spirit.
\citet{lorenz2008} compared the $L_1$ and $L_\infty$ norms after rescaling the threshold so that their confidence regions had equal volume, and \citet{li2026} note that lowering their topic weight at a fixed bound enlarges the region of agreement as well as changing its shape.
We extend this in two steps.
Matching \emph{coverage}, the share of actual opinion pairs a gate admits, rather than geometric volume, accounts for where opinions are and for the boundaries of the opinion space, and it is the only option for angular gates, whose regions have no finite volume.
Coverage then drifts as opinions move, so matching it once, at the start, is not the end of the matter.

The other four choices matter as much.
In an agent-based model of AI governance we reported, in an unpublished draft, that geometry \emph{reverses} the effect of enforcement lag on shocks.
The finding came from reading each geometry against a fixed benchmark; the test that actually compares the geometries says they differ in the same direction in the draft's model, in the audited one and in every version in between (Section~\ref{sec:case}).
Where the draft placed the origin decided which side of the benchmark one geometry fell, and the threshold, the grouping radius and the stopping time each changed the size of the differences we would have reported.

The comparison of distance measures in multidimensional BC models is a natural instance of a wider problem known from model docking and replication \citep{axtell1996,wilensky2007}: settings that look like implementation details carry substantive assumptions.
Section~\ref{sec:background} sets out the metrics and two facts about them.
Sections~\ref{sec:threshold}--\ref{sec:ruler} take the choices one at a time in a minimal Deffuant model.
Section~\ref{sec:case} returns to the governance model, and Section~\ref{sec:protocol} collects the lessons into an audit list.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Opinions are vectors $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$.
We use three distances,
\begin{align*}
d_{\mathrm{E}}(x_i,x_j) &= \lVert x_i - x_j \rVert_2, &
d_{\cos}(x_i,x_j) &= 1 - \cos\theta_{ij}, &
d_\angle(x_i,x_j) &= \theta_{ij},
\end{align*}
where $\theta_{ij}$ is the angle between $x_i$ and $x_j$.
Two agents may interact when $d(x_i,x_j) \le \varepsilon$.
When agent $i$ hears agent $j$ it moves a fraction $r$ of the way toward $j$.
Writing $u = x/\lVert x\rVert$,
\begin{align*}
\text{Euclidean:}\quad & x_i \leftarrow x_i + r\,(x_j - x_i),\\
\text{cosine (chordal):}\quad & x_i \leftarrow \lVert x_i\rVert\,\frac{u_i + r(u_j - u_i)}{\lVert u_i + r(u_j - u_i)\rVert},\\
\text{angular (geodesic):}\quad & x_i \leftarrow \lVert x_i\rVert\,\bigl(\cos(r\theta_{ij})\,u_i + \sin(r\theta_{ij})\,t_{ij}\bigr),
\end{align*}
with $t_{ij}$ the unit tangent from $u_i$ toward $u_j$.
Both angular steps keep the length of $x_i$; an agent at the origin has no direction and does not move.
After each step, opinions are clipped back into the opinion cube; in the experiments below, clipping altered \VClipMean\ of angular updates on average (at most \VClipMax\ in any run), so the angular steps are close to length-preserving in practice.

\begin{lemma}[Monotone equivalence]
\label{lem:monotone}
If one distance is a strictly increasing function of another, $d' = f(d)$, then the gate $d \le \varepsilon$ and the gate $d' \le f(\varepsilon)$ admit exactly the same pairs.
In particular the cosine gate at $\varepsilon$, the angular gate at $\arccos(1-\varepsilon)$, and the Euclidean gate on normalised vectors at $\sqrt{2\varepsilon}$ (since $\lVert u_i - u_j\rVert^2 = 2 d_{\cos}$) are one gate.
\end{lemma}
\noindent So once their thresholds are matched, two monotonically related metrics can differ only through their update rules and through how outcomes are measured.

\paragraph{The origin.}
Euclidean distance and the Euclidean step are unchanged by an affine change of coordinates $x \mapsto a x + b$ (with $\varepsilon$ scaled by $|a|$).
Angular distances and angular steps are not: they measure direction from the origin.
If opinions are coded in $[0,1]^d$, every opinion lies in one orthant and an angular gate measures direction as seen from the corner where every dimension is at its minimum.
The same holds for any multiplicative step elsewhere in a model, such as shrinking a signal by trust, or decay toward zero: it pulls toward the origin, so the origin's meaning becomes part of the model.
A model is free of this choice only if every step is equivariant under the change of coordinates the modeller considers arbitrary.

\section{Choice 1: the threshold value}
\label{sec:threshold}

A gate's \emph{coverage} is the share of pairs it admits.
For opinions drawn independently and uniformly, which is how most BC studies start, coverage at the start is the same for network neighbours as for random pairs and can be computed directly (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}, Table~\ref{tab:coverage}).

In the unit cube a cosine gate barely restricts anything: at $\varepsilon = 0.5$ it admits \UnitFourCos\ of pairs in four dimensions against \UnitFourEuc\ for Euclidean distance, and \UnitSixteenCos\ against \UnitSixteenEuc\ at $d=16$.
Centring removes the orthant effect but does not make one number mean one thing.
The pattern follows from concentration of distances in high dimension \citep{aggarwal2001}.

\begin{proposition}[Coverage at a fixed threshold]
\label{prop:concentration}
For independent uniform opinions, as $d \to \infty$:
(i) $d_{\mathrm{E}}/\sqrt{d}$ converges to $1/\sqrt6$ in $[0,1]^d$ and to $\sqrt{2/3}$ in $[-1,1]^d$;
(ii) $\cos\theta$ converges to $3/4$ in $[0,1]^d$, so $d_{\cos} \to 1/4$ and $\theta \to \arccos(3/4) \approx 0.72$;
(iii) in $[-1,1]^d$, $\cos\theta \to 0$ with fluctuations of order $1/\sqrt d$, so $\theta \to \pi/2$.
Hence for any fixed $\varepsilon$, coverage under each metric tends to 0 or 1 according to which side of the limit $\varepsilon$ falls; the exception is a threshold exactly at the limit, such as cosine at $\varepsilon = 1$ in the centred cube, which admits half of all pairs in every dimension by the symmetry $x \mapsto -x$.
\end{proposition}
\noindent (i) and (ii) follow from the law of large numbers applied to $\sum_k (x_k - y_k)^2$, $\sum_k x_k y_k$ and $\sum_k x_k^2$ with $\mathbb{E}[x_k]=1/2$, $\mathbb{E}[x_k^2]=1/3$ in $[0,1]$; (iii) from the same sums with mean zero.
Table~\ref{tab:coverage} shows the approach to these limits.
To admit 15\% of pairs in $[0,1]^4$, Euclidean distance needs $\varepsilon = \MatchUnitEuc$, cosine $\MatchUnitCos$ and angular $\MatchUnitAng$; in $[-1,1]^4$ the values are \MatchCentredEuc, \MatchCentredCos\ and \MatchCentredAng.

\section{Choice 2: when coverage is matched}
\label{sec:matching}

\subsection{Design}
We use a minimal Deffuant model (Appendix~\ref{app:odd}): 500 agents on an Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph, random matchings each round, $\mu = 0.3$.
The factorial crosses the opinion space (unit or centred), the dimension ($d \in \{2,4,8\}$) and a reference coverage $c \in \{5\%, 15\%, 30\%\}$: 18 cells.
In each cell Euclidean distance runs at the $\varepsilon$ that admits a share $c$ of pairs at the start, and cosine and angular distance each run under three threshold rules:
\begin{description}
\item[shared:] the same number as Euclidean's $\varepsilon$;
\item[matched at the start:] the $\varepsilon$ that admits the same share $c$ of pairs at $t = 0$;
\item[matched over the run:] the $\varepsilon$ whose pass rate over the first 2{,}000 rounds equals Euclidean's, found by bisection on 50 pilot seeds that are not reused. All \VCalTotal\ calibrations met their tolerance of 0.005 (largest error \VCalMaxErr), and the pass rate rose with $\varepsilon$ at every step (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:calibration}).
\end{description}
Each cell has 100 seeds, paired across metrics and rules.
Euclidean dynamics are unchanged by shifting and rescaling the cube, so its 18 cells are 9 distinct designs, each run in both coordinate systems.
Statistics were fixed before the runs were analysed.
Gaps to the Euclidean run are differences for the pass rate and $\log_2$ ratios for everything else, with paired bootstrap 95\% intervals.
Summaries across cases are medians of absolute gaps; the signed gaps are in the result tables.
We call a gap \emph{equivalent to zero} when its 90\% interval lies within $\pm 0.02$ for the pass rate or $\pm 0.25$ on the $\log_2$ scale, a two-one-sided-tests criterion \citep{lakens2017}.
The primary outcome is the number of opinion groups holding at least 1\% of agents, counted with one ruler for every metric (Euclidean distance in centred coordinates, radius 5\% of the cube's diagonal) after 2{,}000 rounds.
Cosine and angular results are reported separately; there are \VCases\ metric--cell pairs per rule.

\paragraph{A shared threshold changes how often agents talk.}
With one shared $\varepsilon$, the realised pass rate differs from Euclidean's by a median of \VRawPassGap\ (a share of attempted conversations), and is equivalent to Euclidean's in \VRawPassEquiv\ of \VCases\ cases.

\paragraph{Matching at the start does not fix the interaction rate.}
Matched at $t=0$, the median pass-rate gap is still \VTzeroPassGap\ (\VTzeroPassEquiv\ of \VCases\ equivalent): coverage drifts as opinions converge, faster under Euclidean distance than under the angular metrics, whose steps rotate an opinion without shortening it.

\paragraph{Matching over the run fixes the interaction rate, not the group structure.}
Matched over the run, the pass rates agree (median gap \VRunPassGap; \VRunPassEquiv\ of \VCases\ equivalent).
The number of opinion groups does not: its median gap is \VRunGroupGap\ on the $\log_2$ scale, and only \VRunGroupEquiv\ of \VCases\ cases are equivalent, fewer than under a shared threshold (\VRawGroupEquiv).
Figure~\ref{fig:curves} shows why no single matching rule settles the question: at equal realised coverage, the metrics' curves do not coincide, so the gap depends on where on the curves one compares them.
An earlier version of this analysis reported that over-the-run matching removed the group gap; it used the effective number of groups, which is dominated by single agents left between groups and which, once groups have formed, is nearly fixed by the pass rate itself.
We now treat matching as a choice of estimand, not as a correction: matched at the start, a comparison includes a metric's effect on how often agents interact; matched over the run, it excludes it.

\paragraph{What remains comes partly from the gate, partly from the update rule.}
Matched over the run, the angular metrics still leave opinions more spread out than Euclidean distance does (median variance gap \VRunVarGap; spread in direction \VRunDispGap; \VRunVarEquiv\ and \VRunDispEquiv\ of \VCases\ cases equivalent).
Crossing the gate with the update rule separates the two sources.
Averaged over the 18 cells, and relative to the pure Euclidean pairing, a Euclidean gate with the cosine step raises the variance of final opinions by \VXEucCosVar\ ($\log_2$, 95\% CI), most of the \VXCosCosVar\ of the pure cosine pairing, while a cosine gate with the Euclidean step raises it by only \VXCosEucVar.
The spread of opinion \emph{directions} behaves the other way round: it follows the gate (\VXCosEucDisp\ for the cosine gate with the Euclidean step, against \VXEucCosDisp\ for the reverse).
So the overall spread is mostly the update rule's doing and the directional fragmentation mostly the gate's (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:crossing}); a comparison that changes both at once cannot tell them apart.

\paragraph{Cosine and angular gates are one gate.}
Matched at the start, cosine and angular runs differ in realised pass rate by at most \VCosAngPassMax, and their group counts under the common ruler differ by a median of \VCosAngCommon\ (at most \VCosAngCommonMax), as Lemma~\ref{lem:monotone} requires.


\section{Choice 5: the ruler and the stopping time}
\label{sec:ruler}

How groups are counted and when a run is stopped are rarely reported, and both can manufacture or hide a difference.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig3_ruler_horizon.pdf}
\caption{(a) Cosine against angular, matched at the start, so the two gates admit the same pairs: median $|\log_2|$ gap in the number of opinion groups when groups are joined within a radius that is a fraction of each metric's own $\varepsilon$, and with one common ruler. (b) Median $|\log_2|$ gap to Euclidean in the number of groups (cosine) by run length, with the share of Euclidean runs that have not settled.}
\label{fig:ruler}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The ruler.}
Joining final opinions that lie within a fraction of the run's own $\varepsilon$ is a natural rule, and the one our first draft used.
It gives two gates that admit the same pairs different group counts, because $0.1\varepsilon$ in cosine units is about three times wider in angle than $0.1\varepsilon$ in radians ($d_{\cos} \approx \theta^2/2$).
Under such rulers, cosine and angular runs matched at the start differ by a median of \VCosAngOwn\ in $\log_2$ group count, and by up to \VCosAngOwnMax; under the common ruler, by \VCosAngCommon\ (Figure~\ref{fig:ruler}a).
The apparent difference between the two gates was a property of the ruler.

\paragraph{The stopping time.}
After 500 rounds, \VUnsettledFive\ of Euclidean runs are still changing; after 2{,}000, \VUnsettledTwo; after 4{,}000, \VUnsettledFour.
The group gaps barely move over the same range (matched at the start: \VHorTzeroFive\ to \VHorTzeroFour; matched over the run: \VHorRunFive\ to \VHorRunFour; Figure~\ref{fig:ruler}b), so the remaining geometry effect on group structure is not a transient here.
It could have been: reporting the stopping time, and checking a longer one, is what lets a reader tell.


\section{Choices 3 and 4 in a governance model: the origin and the test}
\label{sec:case}

\subsection{The model and the claim}
PolicySwarm \citep{policyswarm} is an agent-based model of how a population's views on AI regulation respond to a policy, to media and to shocks.
Agents hold four-dimensional opinions (deployment caps, transparency, enforcement, audit intensity) on a Barab\'asi--Albert network.
Each tick combines bounded-confidence peer influence, a media pull, random shocks to some agents, and a pull toward the policy as each agent perceives it.
Agent $i$ perceives the policy $p$ as $\tau_i\,(p + b_i) + \eta$, where $\tau_i \in [0,1]$ is its trust in the regulator, $b_i$ an ideological bias and $\eta$ noise.
The experiment we use estimates how much a schedule of shocks changes the final variance of opinions, from paired runs with and without shocks, at enforcement lags of 0, 20 and 40 ticks; its outcome is the shock effect at lag 40 indexed to lag 0 $= 100$.

An unpublished draft of ours reported that geometry \emph{reverses} the effect of lag: the index rose to about 130 under Euclidean distance and fell to about 75 under cosine distance.
Read as a policy result, it said that delays in enforcement make a population more fragile after shocks under one model of opinion similarity and less fragile under another, so the advice on whether delay is costly would hinge on an untested modelling choice.
An internal audit before any external use replaced the draft's engine; this section uses the audited engine, with the draft's choices restored as switches, and the draft's own engine for the backward check.

\subsection{Design}
\paragraph{Forward.} In the audited engine we switch three choices independently: \emph{gate space} (the gate and the angular steps act on unit coordinates, measuring angles from the cube's corner, or on centred ones), \emph{threshold} (shared, matched at the start, or matched over the run on pilot seeds), and \emph{policy pull} (gated, applied only after a conversation that passes the gate, as in the draft; or an ungated anchor). Trust shrinks toward the centre of the cube, as in the audited engine.
\paragraph{Backward.} In the draft's engine we undo the audit's other changes (the cosine step, the random streams, the tightening tick) in all eight combinations, and then the trust step.
\paragraph{Where trust shrinks.} With the draft's gate (shared threshold, gated pull) we cross the trust target, the cube's corner ($-1$ on every dimension, the draft's origin) or its centre, with the gate space, and add a control in which every agent's perceived policy is moved by the same amount, $-(1-\bar\tau)$, the average of the corner's shift.
Shrinking toward the corner is shrinking toward the centre plus a shift of $-(1-\tau_i)$ that depends on each agent's trust, so the control separates a change in the mean perceived policy from a change that differs between trusting and distrustful agents.
\paragraph{Which target.} Trust has to shrink toward something; we treat the target as a parameter and run four, the corner, the centre, the population's mean starting opinion (status quo) and the agent's own current opinion, both in the draft's setting and in the recommended one (centred gate, threshold matched over the run and recalibrated for each target, anchor pull).

Every cell crosses the two geometries with the three lags, with 100 paired seeds.
For every cell we report the index for each geometry with a bootstrap 95\% interval, the absolute lag-0 effect, and the paired interaction: Euclidean's lag contrast in the relative shock effect minus cosine's, so that a positive value means cosine \emph{attenuates} the lag effect.

\paragraph{Choice 4: the test.}
Across all \VCaseCells\ configurations, the interaction is positive in \VCasePositive, indistinguishable from zero in \VCaseNull\ and negative in \VCaseNegative\ (Figure~\ref{fig:interaction}).
In the draft's own engine and settings it is \VCDraftInter\ \VCDraftCI: the geometries differ in the same direction as in the audited model.
What looked like a reversal was cosine's index falling below 100 (\VCDraftCosIdx, against \VCDraftEucIdx\ for Euclidean) while the comparison between geometries kept its sign.
Reading each geometry against 100 asks whether each effect grows with lag; the claim was about the difference between geometries, and only the interaction tests that \citep{gelman2006}.
Two of the negative cells are the unit-cube cells with a matched threshold and the gated pull, where the matched cosine thresholds are tiny because all opinion vectors point into one orthant; the third is a different trust target (below). One of the first two has the largest index in the study (\VCUnitRunGatedCosIdx), whose lag-0 effect is only \VCUnitRunGatedCosZero, so a small absolute change makes a large ratio.

\paragraph{The target is an assumption.}
Trust has to shrink the perceived policy toward something, and the four targets give different answers (Table~\ref{tab:anchors}).
The status quo behaves like the centre (Euclidean \VTCentredRunStatusQuoEucIdx, cosine \VTCentredRunStatusQuoCosIdx\ in the recommended design), as it must when starting opinions are uniform and their mean is the centre.
The other two targets change the headline for both geometries.
Shrinking toward the corner lowers Euclidean's index from \VTCentredRunCentreEucIdx\ to \VTCentredRunCornerEucIdx\ \VTCentredRunCornerEucIdxCI\ and puts cosine's at \VTCentredRunCornerCosIdx\ \VTCentredRunCornerCosIdxCI: under that assumption lag has no effect under cosine distance, even with a centred gate and a matched threshold.
Shrinking toward the agent's own opinion, so that trust sets how far an agent moves toward the policy, gives Euclidean \VTCentredRunOwnEucIdx\ and cosine \VTCentredRunOwnCosIdx, with an interaction of \VTCentredRunOwnInter\ \VTCentredRunOwnCI; in the draft's setting the same target makes it negative, \VTUnitRawOwnInter\ \VTUnitRawOwnCI, so that cosine amplifies the lag effect more.
The comparison between geometries therefore keeps its sign under three of the four targets, not all of them, and the size of the lag effect itself, the number a policy reader would use, runs from \VTCentredRunCornerEucIdx\ to \VTCentredRunStatusQuoEucIdx\ under Euclidean distance across the four.
Which target is right is a substantive claim about how distrust distorts a policy signal; the model cannot settle it, so it has to be stated and varied.

\paragraph{The residual effect.}
In the recommended design with trust shrinking toward the centre, the geometry effect does not vanish: Euclidean's index is \VCRecommendedEucIdx\ and cosine's \VCRecommendedCosIdx, with interaction \VCRecommendedInter\ \VCRecommendedCI.
Both geometries amplify the effect of lag, and cosine amplifies it less.
That is the result we would now report, together with the condition the target sweep attaches to it: it holds for the centre and the status quo, shrinks under the corner and disappears when trust scales each agent's own movement.


\section{An audit list for comparisons of distance measures}
\label{sec:protocol}

\begin{framed}
\noindent\textbf{Before reporting that a distance measure changes a result}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Audit the origin.} Recode the opinion space by an affine shift and rerun a few seeds; any outcome that changes depends on the origin. Centre the space at a meaningful neutral point, or say why not, and treat every shrinkage or decay target (trust, attention, forgetting) as an explicit, varied assumption.
\item \textbf{Do not rely on a shared threshold value.} A shared $\varepsilon$ admits a different share of pairs under each metric. If one is used, show that realised coverage matches.
\item \textbf{Report the response to $\varepsilon$, not one matched point.} Plot outcomes against \emph{realised} coverage across a range of thresholds. A point matched at the start and a point matched over the run answer different questions (the effect including, or excluding, the metric's effect on how often agents interact); name the one you report.
\item \textbf{Check for monotone equivalence.} Metrics related by a strictly increasing function are one gate once matched (Lemma~\ref{lem:monotone}); differences between them come from the update rule or the measurement.
\item \textbf{Test the comparison.} A claim that a metric changes an effect is an interaction; test it directly \citep{gelman2006}, and support ``no difference'' with an equivalence test \citep{lakens2017}, not a non-significant one.
\item \textbf{Report the ruler and the stopping time.} Count groups with one rule for every metric, report the share of runs that have not settled, and show that conclusions survive a longer run.
\end{enumerate}
\end{framed}

\section{Limitations}
Opinions start uniform and independent; clustered or correlated starting opinions change how coverage depends on the metric, though not the argument for reporting it.
We study pairwise Deffuant updates only; for Hegselmann--Krause averaging the natural analogue of coverage is the mean number of neighbours inside the confidence region, which we did not test.
The case study rests on one model; its trust mechanism is specific to it, while the general point (multiplicative steps inherit the meaning of the origin) is not.
Our list of hidden choices is not exhaustive: the network, the update order and the size of the population are choices of the same kind.

\section{ODD description of the minimal model}
\label{app:odd}
\paragraph{Purpose and patterns.} To isolate how the confidence gate's distance measure, its threshold and the measurement of outcomes affect bounded-confidence dynamics. The model is judged by whether it reproduces consensus at a wide bound and fragmentation at a narrow one.

\paragraph{Entities, state variables and scales.} Agents, each with an opinion $x_i \in [0,1]^d$ or $[-1,1]^d$. A fixed undirected Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph with $n=500$ nodes and $5n$ edges (mean degree 10), without self-loops or duplicate edges. Time is in rounds; runs last up to 4{,}000 rounds, with outcomes recorded after 500, 1{,}000, 2{,}000 and 4{,}000.

\paragraph{Process overview and scheduling.} In each round: (1) draw a random matching (an edge is kept when it comes first, in a random order, among all edges touching either endpoint); (2) evaluate the gate for every matched pair; (3) update both agents of every pair that passes, from their previous opinions, with rate $\mu = 0.3$, and clip to the cube.

\paragraph{Design concepts.} \emph{Interaction:} pairwise, along graph edges. \emph{Stochasticity:} the graph and the initial opinions from one seeded stream, the matchings from a second, so runs that share a seed differ only in the gate, the update rule and $\varepsilon$. \emph{Observation:} see Section~\ref{sec:matching}.

\paragraph{Initialisation.} Independent uniform opinions on the cube.

\paragraph{Input data.} None.

\paragraph{Submodels.} The updates of Section~\ref{sec:background}. The gate and the update rule can be set separately (Section~\ref{sec:matching}).
